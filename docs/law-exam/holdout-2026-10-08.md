Sums paid before discharge are recoverable from the party who received them, and sums payable cease to be payable (s. 3 (1)). So, under the Act's default rule, the $8,000 deposit is recoverable and the $10,000 balance is no longer payable. If the venue incurred expenses in connection with performing the contract before discharge, the court, if it considers it just having regard to all the circumstances, may allow it to retain or recover all or part of the sums paid or payable up to the amount of those expenses, including a reasonable sum for overhead and for work or services performed personally (s. 3 (2)); the custom signs and planning time are the kind of thing the venue would put forward. In deciding what is recovered or retained, the court does not take into account insurance money that became payable because of the frustrating event, unless there was an obligation to insure in an express term of the contract or under an enactment (s. 3 (5)). The non-refundable clause matters: where the contract contains a provision that, on its true construction, is intended to have effect in the event of circumstances that would frustrate the contract, the court gives effect to the provision and applies s. 3 only to the extent consistent with it (s. 3 (6)). How that clause is construed is therefore central.
