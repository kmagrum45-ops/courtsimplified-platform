## ALL COURTS

### E01 — pintea-v-johns
- courts: small_claims, civil, family
- topic: Fair treatment of self-represented people
- case: Pintea v. Johns
- citations: 2017 SCC 23; [2017] 1 S.C.R. 470
- court: Supreme Court of Canada; decided 2017-04-18
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2017/2017scc23/2017scc23.html
- extra_link: Canadian Judicial Council, Statement of Principles on Self-represented Litigants and Accused Persons (2006): https://cjc-ccm.ca/sites/default/files/documents/2020/Final-Statement-of-Principles-SRL.pdf
- summary: The Supreme Court endorsed the Canadian Judicial Council's Statement of Principles on Self-represented Litigants and Accused Persons (2006) (para 4). The Statement is advisory, not a binding code. It says judges should, for example, ask whether self-represented people understand their options, explain the relevant law where appropriate before critical choices, not deny relief over minor or easily fixed flaws, and make sure procedural and evidentiary rules don't unjustly hinder them. It also says self-represented people must learn the procedures, prepare their case and treat the court with respect, and that there is no duty to help someone who is disrespectful, vexatious or making no reasonable effort. The decision itself also holds that contempt requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the person actually knew of the order (paras 1–3).
- cautions: The Statement's points are guidance to judges, not rights a self-represented person can demand.
- review: confirmed 9:00 a.m.; checked paras 1–4 (whole decision) against CanLII PDF; Statement read from the Canadian Judicial Council's website. Correction made: the fair-hearing duties come from the Statement, not the Court's own words.

### E02 — grant-thornton-v-new-brunswick
- courts: small_claims, civil, family
- topic: When the 2-year limitation clock starts (discoverability)
- case: Grant Thornton LLP v. New Brunswick
- citations: 2021 SCC 31; [2021] 2 S.C.R. 704
- court: Supreme Court of Canada; decided 2021-07-29
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2021/2021scc31/2021scc31.html
- statute_link: Limitations Act, 2002 (Ontario): https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/02l24
- summary: A claim is "discovered" when the person knows, or reasonably ought to know, the material facts from which a plausible inference can be drawn that the other side is liable (para 42). All the facts the statute lists are needed, so knowing of a loss alone is not enough (para 43). This means more than suspicion, but not certainty. The person does not need to know the full extent of the harm or its exact cause (para 46), and in negligence does not need to know the legal elements such as the standard of care (para 48). Suspicion can trigger a duty to look into it with reasonable diligence (para 44). The case is from New Brunswick, but the Court said its test is the same as Ontario's (paras 35, 45). In Ontario, the Limitations Act, 2002 adds a fourth element: that a court proceeding is an appropriate means to remedy the loss (s. 5(1)(a)(iv)). The person is presumed to have known on the day the act or omission happened, unless they prove otherwise (s. 5(2)). The basic deadline is two years from discovery (s. 4).
- cautions: (none beyond the summary)
- review: confirmed 9:05 a.m.; checked paras 35, 42–48 against CanLII PDF; Limitations Act, 2002 ss. 4, 5(1)–(2) checked on e-Laws (version from 4 Dec 2024).

### E03 — enbridge-v-froese
- courts: small_claims, civil, family
- topic: Missed deadline to appeal: extending time
- case: Enbridge Gas Distribution Inc. v. Froese
- citations: 2013 ONCA 131; 114 O.R. (3d) 636
- court: Court of Appeal for Ontario (Gillese J.A., in chambers); decided 2013-03-04
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2013/2013onca131/2013onca131.html
- summary: If the deadline to appeal was missed, the question is whether the "justice of the case" requires an extension of time. The court considers all relevant factors, including whether the person meant to appeal within the time limit, how long the delay was and why, prejudice to the other side from the delay, and the merits of the proposed appeal (para 15). Lack of merit alone can be enough to refuse an extension, especially where permission (leave) to appeal is needed (para 16). Where a case has already been appealed once to the Divisional Court, a further appeal needs leave, and that court's decision is generally meant to be final (paras 19–22).
- cautions: This is a Court of Appeal decision. Whether the Divisional Court uses the same test for its own appeal deadlines has not yet been checked.
- review: confirmed 12:21 p.m.; checked paras 15–16, 19–22 (whole decision read) against CanLII PDF; appeal status checked by CanLII search.

## SMALL CLAIMS COURT

### E04 — van-de-vrande-v-butkowsky
- courts: small_claims
- topic: Rule 12.02: ending a case early
- case: Van de Vrande v. Butkowsky
- citations: 2010 ONCA 230; 99 O.R. (3d) 641
- court: Court of Appeal for Ontario; decided 2010-03-30
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2010/2010onca230/2010onca230.html
- rule_link: Rules of the Small Claims Court, O. Reg. 258/98: https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/980258
- summary: Small Claims Court has no summary judgment; the rules left it out on purpose (para 11). Instead, Rule 12.02 lets the court strike out or amend all or part of any document, including a claim or defence, that discloses no reasonable cause of action or defence, may delay or make it difficult to have a fair trial, or is inflammatory, a waste of time, a nuisance or an abuse of process (paras 14–15). It is not a full summary judgment motion with extensive affidavits, but unlike the civil rule, evidence is not barred (paras 17, 19). The grounds "inflammatory, a waste of time, a nuisance" set a lower bar than the civil rule's "frivolous, vexatious or abuse of process" (para 20). Because these motions are often brought and answered by self-represented people, the test should stay simple and avoid complex civil case law (para 21). If a claim is struck, the court can dismiss or stay the action, or impose terms.
- cautions: (none beyond the summary)

## CIVIL (SUPERIOR COURT)

### E08 — hryniak-v-mauldin
- courts: civil
- topic: Rule 20: summary judgment
- case: Hryniak v. Mauldin
- citations: 2014 SCC 7; [2014] 1 S.C.R. 87
- court: Supreme Court of Canada; decided 2014-01-23
- link_hint: https://canlii.ca/t/g2s18
- summary: Summary judgment must be granted when there is no genuine issue requiring a trial (para 47). That's the case when the judge can fairly decide the case on the motion: find the facts, apply the law, and do it in a way that is proportionate, faster and cheaper than a trial (para 49). The judge first decides on the evidence filed, without the extra powers. If a trial still seems needed, the judge can weigh evidence, assess credibility, draw inferences or hear some oral evidence to avoid one, unless that would be against the interest of justice (para 66).

### E09 — mountain-view-farms-v-mcqueen
- courts: civil
- topic: Rule 19.08: setting aside default judgment
- case: Mountain View Farms Ltd. v. McQueen
- citations: 2014 ONCA 194; 119 O.R. (3d) 561
- court: Court of Appeal for Ontario; decided 2014-03-14
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2014/2014onca194/2014onca194.html
- summary: The question is whether the interests of justice favour setting aside the default judgment (para 47). The court considers whether the motion was brought promptly after the defendant learned of the judgment, whether there is a plausible excuse for the default, and whether the defendant has an arguable defence on the merits (para 48). It also considers the prejudice to each side and the effect on the overall integrity of the justice system (para 49). These are not rigid rules (para 50). An arguable defence can justify setting the judgment aside even if the other factors aren't fully met. The defence doesn't have to be sure to win, but it must have an "air of reality" (para 51). On this motion, the judge decides only whether there is an arguable defence, not whether it succeeds (paras 61–63).
- cautions: (none)

### E10 — imperial-tobacco
- courts: civil
- topic: Rule 21.01(1)(b): striking a claim
- case: R. v. Imperial Tobacco Canada Ltd.
- citations: 2011 SCC 42; [2011] 3 S.C.R. 45
- court: Supreme Court of Canada; decided 2011-07-29
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2011/2011scc42/2011scc42.html
- summary: A claim is struck only if it is plain and obvious, assuming the facts pleaded are true, that it has no reasonable prospect of success. If there is a reasonable prospect, it goes to trial (para 17). Judges apply this generously: a new kind of claim that is arguable should be allowed to proceed (para 21). The motion looks only at the claim as written, not at evidence. So the person suing must clearly state the facts they rely on (para 22). If new facts come up later, the answer is to amend the claim (paras 23–24).
- cautions: The case arose under British Columbia's rule; Ontario courts apply the same test under Rule 21.01(1)(b), and Ontario's Rule 21.01(2)(b) bars evidence on these motions.
- review: confirmed 8:08 a.m.; checked paras 17, 21–24 against CanLII PDF.

### E11 — hb-fuller-v-rogers
- courts: civil
- topic: Rule 48.14 / 37.14: setting aside a registrar's dismissal for delay
- case: H.B. Fuller Company v. Rogers (Rogers Law Office)
- citations: 2015 ONCA 173; 386 D.L.R. (4th) 262
- court: Court of Appeal for Ontario; decided 2015-03-16
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2015/2015onca173/2015onca173.html
- summary: When a registrar has dismissed a case for delay, the court weighs four factors: how long the delay was and whether it is adequately explained, whether missing the deadline was inadvertent, whether the motion to set aside was brought promptly, and whether the delay prejudiced the defendant (para 20). Not every factor has to be met. The court looks at all the circumstances, including the defendant's conduct, to reach a just result (paras 21–23). Courts generally prefer to decide cases on their merits, especially when the delay was a lawyer's mistake (paras 26–27). But they also weigh whether the defendant suffered prejudice that money can't fix, whether a fair trial is still possible, and the value of finality (para 28). This case applied the former Rule 48.14. Since January 1, 2015, the rule dismisses a case not set down for trial or otherwise ended within five years of being started.
- cautions: The case applied the former Rule 48.14; use the current rule's timeline.
- review: confirmed 8:13 a.m.; checked paras 20–23, 26–28 and footnote 2 against CanLII PDF; still applied under the current rule (Greyson v. Echelon, 2026 ONSC 6358; Cripps v. Thunder Bay, 2026 ONSC 3864; Prescott v. Barbon, 2018 ONCA 504).

### E12 — boucher-v-public-accountants-council
- courts: civil
- topic: Rule 57: how much costs
- case: Boucher v. Public Accountants Council for the Province of Ontario
- citations: (2004), 71 O.R. (3d) 291 (C.A.); 2004 CanLII 14579 (ON CA)
- court: Court of Appeal for Ontario; decided 2004-06-22
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2004/2004canlii14579/2004canlii14579.html
- summary: Costs are not just hours times hourly rates. The judge can do that calculation but must then step back and ask whether the result is fair and reasonable in all the circumstances (para 24). The goal is an amount that is fair and reasonable for the losing party to pay in that case, not the winner's actual costs (para 26). What the parties could reasonably expect to pay is relevant (para 38). Very large awards can discourage people from going to court, which works against access to justice (para 37). Partial indemnity costs that are nearly the same as substantial indemnity costs are an error in principle (para 36).

### E13 — fong-v-chan
- courts: civil
- topic: Rule 57: costs for self-represented people
- case: Fong v. Chan
- citations: (1999), 46 O.R. (3d) 330 (C.A.); 1999 CanLII 2052 (ON CA)
- court: Court of Appeal for Ontario; decided 1999-12-08
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/1999/1999canlii2052/1999canlii2052.html
- summary: A self-represented person can be awarded costs, but there is no automatic right; it is up to the judge (paras 21–25). Costs are not calculated the same way as for someone with a lawyer. There is nothing for time any litigant would have to spend anyway. Costs are only for work a lawyer would normally do, and only if the person shows they gave up income-earning activity to do it. Even then, the allowance is only "moderate" or "reasonable" (para 26). The other side cannot ignore a self-represented person's reasonable settlement offer without the usual costs consequences (para 24).
- cautions: (none)

### E14 — rjr-macdonald
- courts: civil
- topic: Rule 40: temporary injunction
- case: RJR-MacDonald Inc. v. Canada (Attorney General)
- citations: [1994] 1 S.C.R. 311; 1994 CanLII 117 (SCC)
- court: Supreme Court of Canada; decided 1994-03-03
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/1994/1994canlii117/1994canlii117.html
- summary: There are three steps, for both a temporary injunction and a stay: (1) a serious question to be tried, (2) irreparable harm to the person asking if the order is refused, and (3) which side would suffer more while waiting for a final decision, called the balance of convenience (p. 334). Step 1 is a low bar: the claim only has to be not frivolous or vexatious (pp. 335–337). "Irreparable" is about the kind of harm, not its size. It means harm that can't be measured in money or can't be fixed, often because damages couldn't be collected from the other side (p. 341). The public interest can also be weighed at step 3 (p. 342).
- cautions: References are to S.C.R. page numbers; the decision has no paragraph numbers.
- review: confirmed 8:23 a.m.; checked S.C.R. pp. 334–337, 341–342 (scanned PDF read with text recognition).

### E15 — r-v-cbc
- courts: civil
- topic: Rule 40: injunction ordering someone to act
- case: R. v. Canadian Broadcasting Corp.
- citations: 2018 SCC 5; [2018] 1 S.C.R. 196
- court: Supreme Court of Canada; decided 2018-02-09
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2018/2018scc5/2018scc5.html
- summary: When an injunction would order someone to do something (a mandatory injunction), step 1 of the RJR-MacDonald test is higher. ## FAMILY

### E16 — roberts-v-roberts
- courts: family
- topic: Rule 13: financial disclosure
- case: Roberts v. Roberts
- citations: 2015 ONCA 450
- court: Court of Appeal for Ontario; decided 2015-06-19
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2015/2015onca450/2015onca450.html
- summary: Financial disclosure is the most basic obligation in family law. It is immediate and ongoing (para 11), and automatic: it should not take court orders to get it (para 13). Not disclosing delays the case, puts the other side at a disadvantage, and harms the administration of justice (para 12). If a party breaks a court order, the court can respond under Family Law Rule 1(8), with costs, dismissing a claim, striking documents, refusing further orders, or contempt (para 14). Striking a party's pleadings is used sparingly, only in exceptional cases, such as here, where three disclosure orders were ignored (para 15).
- cautions: When citing Rule 1(8) itself, use the current wording from e-Laws (https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/990114); the decision quotes the 2015 version. No children's issues were involved in this case (para 16); do not suggest the same result automatically applies where children are involved.

### E17 — mattina-v-mattina
- courts: family
- topic: Rule 24: purposes of costs
- case: Mattina v. Mattina
- citations: 2018 ONCA 867
- court: Court of Appeal for Ontario; decided 2018-10-29
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2018/2018onca867/2018onca867.html
- summary: Family costs rules serve four purposes: to partly repay the successful party, to encourage settlement, to discourage and penalize bad behaviour, and to make sure cases are dealt with justly (Rule 2(2)) (para 10). The successful party is presumed to get costs, including in parenting cases, but it is not automatic. The court looks at reasonableness and proportionality, each party's behaviour, time spent, offers to settle, and fees (paras 12–14). A successful party who behaved unreasonably can lose costs or have to pay them (para 16). Full recovery is for specific situations, such as bad faith or beating an offer to settle (paras 15, 18). Civil costs cases should be used with caution in family matters (para 11).
- cautions: Rule 24 was amended in July 2018; cite current rule numbers and wording from e-Laws.

### E18 — beaver-v-hill
- courts: family
- topic: Rule 24: amount of costs
- case: Beaver v. Hill
- citations: 2018 ONCA 840; 143 O.R. (3d) 519
- court: Court of Appeal for Ontario; decided 2018-10-22
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onca/doc/2018/2018onca840/2018onca840.html
- summary: There is no general approach of near-full recovery of costs in family cases. Full recovery is for specific situations, such as bad faith or beating an offer to settle (paras 11, 13). ### E20 — willick-v-willick

- topic: Rule 15: what counts as a material change
- case: Willick v. Willick
- citations: [1994] 3 S.C.R. 670; 1994 CanLII 28 (SCC)

### E21 — lmp-v-ls
- courts: family
- topic: Rule 15: changing spousal support
- case: L.M.P. v. L.S.
- citations: 2011 SCC 64; [2011] 3 S.C.R. 775
- court: Supreme Court of Canada; decided 2011-12-21
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2011/2011scc64/2011scc64.html
- summary: The person asking to change spousal support must show a material change since the last order: a change that, if known at the time, would likely have resulted in different terms (paras 31–32). The test is the same whether or not the order includes the parties' agreement (para 36). The court does not ask whether the original order was right (para 33). If the test is met, the court changes only what that change justifies. It is not a fresh hearing (para 47).
- cautions: Applies to spousal support under Divorce Act s. 17(4.1).

### E22 — michel-v-graydon
- courts: family
- topic: Retroactive child support: disclosure and general principles
- case: Michel v. Graydon
- citations: 2020 SCC 24; [2020] 2 S.C.R. 763
- court: Supreme Court of Canada; reasons released 2020-09-18
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2020/2020scc24/2020scc24.html
- summary: Retroactive child support is not exceptional relief (para 31). It will commonly be appropriate where the paying parent did not disclose increases in income, because that parent holds the information the support amount depends on (para 32). The paying parent has a duty of full and honest disclosure, and non-disclosure is "the cancer of family law litigation" (para 33). Courts should not be quick to find legal barriers to considering retroactive support (para 17). The specific holding, that support can be changed after the child is no longer a child and the order has expired, was decided under British Columbia's Family Law Act, s. 152 (para 9). In Ontario it depends on the statute that applies; see Colucci for the Divorce Act.
- cautions: The jurisdiction holding is under B.C.'s Family Law Act. Never present it as Ontario law. Only the majority reasons (Brown J.) are used.
- review: confirmed 8:50 a.m.; checked paras 9, 17, 31–33 (majority) against CanLII PDF. Correction made: BC holding no longer stated as a general rule.

### E23 — colucci-v-colucci
- courts: family
- topic: Retroactive child support changes (Divorce Act) and cancelling arrears
- case: Colucci v. Colucci
- citations: 2021 SCC 24; [2021] 2 S.C.R. 3
- court: Supreme Court of Canada; decided 2021-06-04
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2021/2021scc24/2021scc24.html
- summary: Under the Divorce Act, a parent asking to change child support retroactively must show a past material change in circumstances. For a decrease, the drop in income must last, be real, and not be by choice (para 113). Support is presumed to change back to the date of "effective notice", but no more than three years before formal notice of the court application. For a decrease, effective notice means clearly telling the other parent and giving the documents that prove the change. For an increase, simply raising the subject is enough (paras 113–114). Without effective notice, the change generally goes back only to formal notice. The court can depart from these dates if the result would be unfair. If the paying parent hid an income increase, the date will generally be when the income rose (para 114). Cancelling arrears is a last resort: the paying parent must show they cannot and never will be able to pay, even with a payment plan (para 138). The court can instead suspend payments or set a payment plan (para 140).
- cautions: Decided under the Divorce Act. Present it as the leading framework for divorced parents; do not claim more for Ontario Family Law Act cases until checked.
- review: confirmed 8:52 a.m.; checked paras 113, 114, 138, 140 against CanLII PDF.

### E24 — barendregt-v-grebliunas
- courts: family

- case: Barendregt v. Grebliunas

- summary: The key question is whether the move is in the child's best interests, considering the child's physical, emotional and psychological safety, security and well-being (para 152). The court considers all the child's circumstances, including the child's views, the history of caregiving, family violence, heritage, and each parent's support for the child's relationship with the other (para 153). In relocation cases it also considers the reasons for the move, its impact on the child, each parent's time and involvement, any existing order or agreement about where the child lives, how reasonable the proposed parenting and travel arrangements are, and each person's compliance with orders (para 154). The court must not consider whether a parent says they would move with or without the child (paras 150, 154). Since March 2021, the Divorce Act sets out these rules, including who bears the burden of proof where an order already exists (s. 16.93) (paras 149–150). This decision refines the older Gordon v. Goertz test.
- cautions: For parents not under the Divorce Act, Ontario's Children's Law Reform Act has its own relocation sections (ss. 39.1–39.4); cite those, not the Divorce Act, for those parents.

### E25 — carey-v-laiken
- courts: family, civil
- topic: Rule 31 (family) / contempt of a court order (family and civil)
- case: Carey v. Laiken
- citations: 2015 SCC 17; [2015] 2 S.C.R. 79
- court: Supreme Court of Canada; decided 2015-04-16
- link_hint: https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2015/2015scc17/2015scc17.html
- summary: Civil contempt has three elements, each proven beyond a reasonable doubt (para 32): the order states clearly and unequivocally what should and should not be done (para 33); the person actually knew about it, which can be inferred or shown by wilful blindness (para 34); and they intentionally did what it forbids or failed to do what it requires (para 35). It need not be proven that they meant to disobey; their motive goes to the penalty (para 38). Contempt is discretionary and a last resort, not a routine way to enforce orders (para 36). A judge may decline to find contempt where the person acted in good faith and took reasonable steps to comply (para 37).
